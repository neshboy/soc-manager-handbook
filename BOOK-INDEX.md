# SOC Manager's Operating Handbook

**BOOK-INDEX.md — canonical part list and appendix list.**
**Status:** Draft architecture, Volume 3 of the NESHBOY SOC Professional Library. Companion volumes: *SIGNAL TO ACTION: The Complete SOC Playbook Handbook* (`github.com/neshboy/soc-playbook-handbook`) and *The Detection Engineering Handbook V2* (`github.com/neshboy/detection-engineering-handbook`).

## What this book is

A handbook for the person who runs a SOC — not the analyst working a queue, not the engineer building a rule, but the manager who decides how many analysts there are, what shift they work, whether that rule ships, whether that vendor gets renewed, and what the board is told when it goes wrong. The other two volumes in this series already own enormous, well-developed ground on the technical and playbook-mechanics side of SOC operations. This book deliberately does not re-walk that ground. Its subject is the part of running a SOC that has nothing to do with query syntax or playbook structure: staffing, shift design, hiring, competency and career progression, quality assurance as a *program* rather than a per-ticket check, burnout and attrition, vendor and MSSP contract management, budget and tooling procurement, cross-team politics, the manager's own judgment calls under uncertainty, and crisis leadership from the manager's chair rather than the analyst's or incident commander's.

Every part in this book is written on the assumption that the reader has, or can reach for, the other two volumes for anything technical. Where a topic is genuinely owned by SIGNAL TO ACTION or The Detection Engineering Handbook V2, this book states that plainly in its opening scope paragraph and points there by name and part number rather than re-explaining it thinly. The test applied throughout drafting: if a paragraph could be lifted into either of the other two books without changing a word, it doesn't belong here — cut it and cite instead.

## Multi-level content model

Every part serves multiple readers in the same pages — a shift lead, a SOC director, an HR partner, and a CISO all read this book, at different depths. Content is marked with one of six **content tags**, placed as a bold bracketed label at the start of the paragraph or subsection it governs (never as a heading, never doubled on one paragraph):

- `[CONCEPT]` — foundational "what and why" behind an operating model, program, or decision, with no assumption the reader acts on it directly.
- `[FRONTLINE MANAGER]` — shift-lead/team-lead-facing: day-to-day scheduling, coaching, queue triage, the things a first-line manager actually does this week.
- `[SENIOR MANAGER]` — SOC manager/director-facing: headcount planning, budget defense, vendor negotiation, cross-team politics, risk-acceptance calls.
- `[HR/PEOPLE]` — hiring, performance management, career ladders, burnout/retention, culture — people-operations content that typically requires HR partnership or awareness.
- `[VENDOR/PROCUREMENT]` — tooling selection, MSSP/vendor contract structuring, renewal negotiation, vendor governance.
- `[EXECUTIVE]` — content aimed at the CISO/board-facing conversation: reporting cadence, risk narrative, budget justification, crisis communication upward.

Layered on top are eight **recurring callout features**, each with one fixed Markdown template (defined in `STYLE-GUIDE.md`): **Management Autopsy** (a real/realistic management decision dissected — the decision, why it seemed reasonable, how it failed, the fix), **Manager's Note** (a tactical, practitioner-voice aside — what actually works, said out loud), **Operational Reality** (the gap between the plan/policy and what production SOC operations actually do), **Blind Spot** (a named, specific organizational or programmatic gap — not a technical telemetry gap), **People Risk Trap** (a specific, common people/process risk and its fix), **Field Test** (a concrete, reproducible exercise or drill a manager can run to validate a program actually works), **Cross-Book Pointer** (an explicit "go here for the mechanics" reference into the other two volumes, used whenever a single inline citation isn't enough context), and **What Would Change My Mind** (a falsifiability statement, carried over from the series' epistemic-honesty standard).

## Production model (cross-cutting, applies to every part, template, and figure)

Every chapter and template carries the same mandatory YAML front matter used across the series: `author`, `reviewer` (a different person/agent, mandatory, adversarial not copy-edit), `status` (draft → reviewed → tested → released), `last_validated`, and `depends_on` (the part IDs it assumes, including cross-book dependencies expressed as `soc-playbook:partNN` or `deh:partNN`). Named case studies used inside Management Autopsy boxes and worked examples carry permanent global IDs (`CASE-####`), reusable templates carry `TMPL-####`, and figures carry `FIG-####` — all independent of position in the book and tracked in `CASE-INVENTORY.md`, `TEMPLATE-INVENTORY.md`, and `VISUAL-INVENTORY.md` respectively, so a future reorganization never breaks a cross-reference. A build check fails if a chapter cites another NESHBOY volume without a resolvable entry in Appendix A8's lookup table, or references a `TMPL-####`/`FIG-####` with no corresponding file in the relevant inventory.

## Key structural decisions and provenance

Recorded so future editors know which scope-boundary calls were deliberate, not oversights.

1. **The book is scoped to "people and operations," full stop — anything with a technical detection or playbook-mechanics answer is cross-referenced, never re-derived.** This is the single governing rule behind every part's scope paragraph and the hardest one to hold the line on, because staffing, QA, and performance content constantly brushes up against technical material (a QA review touches escalation quality; a performance conversation touches false-positive engineering). The rule applied consistently: if the paragraph's claim is about a rule, a query, or a playbook's internal structure, it moves to a citation; if the claim is about a person, a process, a contract, or a dollar, it stays.
2. **Quality Assurance (Part 15) is split hard from Escalation Quality (SOC Playbook Handbook, Part 27).** These are the two topics most likely to collapse into one chapter by accident. Escalation Quality defines what a good hand-off looks like, mechanically, for one ticket. QA is the sampling, calibration, and coaching-pipeline program that checks, at scale and over time, whether analysts are actually producing that. Different unit of analysis (one ticket vs. a program), different owner in most orgs (a senior analyst/team lead vs. an HR-adjacent QA function), different failure mode.
3. **Metric *use* (headcount modeling, board narratives, benchmarking) stays here; metric *definitions* stay in SOC Playbook Handbook, Part 32 — Metrics.** Parts 5, 24, and 31 all consume MTTR, handle time, and FP rate as already-defined inputs. Redefining them here risked the exact silent-duplication failure mode the Detection Engineering Handbook V2 rebuild was built to fix — the same number explained slightly differently in two books with no cross-reference.
4. **Alert fatigue is deliberately split by mechanism, not merged into one chapter.** The detection-tuning fix (reduce the false-positive rate that's causing the fatigue) is Detection Engineering Handbook V2, Part 38 — False Positive Engineering. The human/rotation fix (don't leave the same analyst on the highest-noise queue for six consecutive shifts) is this book's Part 17. A manager who only has the second half of that picture will rotate people through a queue that should have been tuned instead; a manager with only the first half will keep tuning a queue whose real problem is that nobody's had a day off in three weeks.
5. **Major-incident content is split three ways on purpose, and none of the three is a duplicate.** SOC Playbook Handbook's Ransomware Master Playbook owns the analyst/incident-commander-level operational playbook. Detection Engineering Handbook V2's applied compromise models (Parts 44–48) own the technical attack-stage analysis. This book's Part 28 owns only what the manager personally does — staffing surge, board communication cadence, retainer activation, decision-authority boundaries — a layer the other two books have no reason to cover because neither is written from the manager's chair.
6. **Three maturity models exist in the series, and Part 30 explicitly refuses to merge them into one.** SOC Playbook Handbook's playbook-maturity model scores playbook quality; Detection Engineering Handbook V2's coverage/quality/debt parts score detection content; this book's Part 30 scores organizational design (staffing, process, training, tooling). A SOC can score well on one and badly on another — collapsing them into a single number would hide exactly the information a manager needs.
7. **AI's staffing and org-design implications (Part 32) are split from AI-assisted SOC operations mechanics (SOC Playbook Handbook, Part 31).** "How does an analyst use an AI copilot during triage" is playbook-mechanics territory already covered. "What does an AI copilot do to my tiering model, my headcount plan, and my obligations to the analysts whose job is changing" had no home in either existing book and is squarely a manager decision — hence its own part rather than a paragraph bolted onto Part 3 or Part 5.
8. **Appendix A8 exists as a standing, build-checked cross-reference index, not a one-time list.** Given how densely this book cites the other two volumes, a static list written once during drafting would drift the moment either companion book revises its part numbering. A8 is treated as a living artifact every new cross-reference must be added to, mirroring the production-model discipline the Detection Engineering Handbook V2 uses for its detection/hunt/figure IDs.
9. **Eight sections, continuous Part 1–33 numbering, mirroring the Detection Engineering Handbook V2's grouping convention.** Chosen for the same reason it was chosen there: labeled sections make reviewer/section-owner assignment visible at a glance, and continuous numbering keeps cross-references from the other two books (and from Appendix A8) stable across drafts.
